The Art of the Wishlist

Reading + speaking · C1 · about 60 minutes
Aims. Main: reading for gist, detail and attitude in a short opinion piece about wishlists. Language: phrases for wanting (have your heart set on, have had your eye on, be dying to, could do with, wouldn’t say no to) and for wishing others well (I hope you…, I wish you + noun, May…, Here’s to…). Productive: building and defending a personal four-gift wishlist.

Anticipated problems. I wish you get the job ✗: Ss transfer from their L1. Contrast I hope you get (real future) with I wish you got (regret, unreal) and I wish you + noun (fixed greeting). could do with = need, not ‘be able to do’. wouldn’t say no to takes a noun or -ing.

Miro board: the same lesson as an interactive board for online classes: open the board.

Stage 1Warm-up

3 min · S–S
Aim: to engage Ss with the topic and get them talking. Ss choose one question each; brief open-class feedback.

Choose a question and discuss it with a partner.

  1. Are you a wishlist person or a surprise person? Which is more fun to give, and which is more fun to get?
  2. What’s the best present you’ve ever received that wasn’t a thing? Why do you still remember it?
  3. What’s something you’ve wanted for years but have never bought for yourself? What’s stopping you?

Stage 2Lead-in

5 min · S–S
Aim: to activate schemata and create a reason to read. Board Ss’ predictions; they check them in Stage 4.

Talk about it

  1. Did you write wishlists as a child? Who were they for?
  2. Is it rude to send people a list of the presents you want?
  3. What’s the difference between a want, a need and a wish?

A gift-giving rule: give just four presents. Something you want, something you need, something to wear and something to read. People use it to avoid clutter and overspending.

    Task 2 · Skim the text 3:00

    Read quickly and answer the two questions below.

    The Art of the Wishlist

    1 There was a time when writing a wishlist meant a letter to Santa, a crayon and a lot of wishful thinking. Today, wishlists live in apps, shared folders and even spreadsheets, complete with links, sizes and colour options. Some people find this wonderfully efficient; others say it has taken the magic out of giving.

    2 Defenders of the modern wishlist have a point. Nobody wants a drawer full of scented candles and novelty socks. A clear list means less waste, fewer returns and no awkward smiles as you unwrap something you will never use. For families on a budget, it also means money goes on things people actually need, not on impulse buys grabbed in a panic on 23 December.

    3 Critics, however, argue that a gift is meant to show that someone knows you. Ticking an item off a list is a far cry from noticing that your friend has been dropping hints about a book for months, or remembering the café you once said you loved. When everything is pre-approved, they say, there is no room left for surprise.

    4 A popular compromise is the ‘four-gift rule’: something you want, something you need, something to wear and something to read. Parents use it to avoid clutter, but many adults have adopted it too, because it forces them to decide what they really have their heart set on rather than listing everything they have ever had their eye on.

    5 Another growing trend is the ‘experience wishlist’. Instead of objects, people ask for concert tickets, a cooking class or simply an afternoon with someone they rarely see. Research on happiness suggests that experiences tend to bring longer-lasting joy than things, partly because we keep retelling the story long after the day itself.

    6 Perhaps the most valuable wishlist is the one we never share. Writing down what we wish for, not only for ourselves but for the people around us, is a quiet way of finding out what really matters. The things on it may be small: more sleep, a call from an old friend, a calmer January. Yet they often reveal more about us than anything we could find in a shop.

      Pronunciation: could do with /kʊd ˈduː wɪð/ (often /kədə wɪð/) · stress: I’m DYing to · I wouldn’t say NO to · HERE’S to · May all your WISHes come TRUE.

      Read the wishlist. Notice the phrases in bold.

      My wishlist, December edition
      I’ve had my eye on a vintage film camera for months, and honestly I’ve got my heart set on it. I could also do with a new winter coat, as mine is falling apart. I wouldn’t say no to a cooking class, and I’m dying to see the Christmas markets in Vienna. As for my friends: I hope you all get some rest, may this winter be kinder than the last, and here’s to a calmer January!

      have had your eye on + noun
      be dying to + infinitive / for + noun
      could do with + noun (= need)
      wouldn’t say no to + noun / -ing (= would like, politely)
      2 · Wishing others wellI hope you + present simple: I hope you get the job!
      I wish you + noun: I wish you a happy new year.
      May + subject + infinitive (formal, toasts): May all your wishes come true.
      Here’s to + noun / -ing: Here’s to a calmer January!
